Water, leaves and optical effects

Explore interference in flowing lines and leaf veins, apparent tilt in a straight grid, facet depth, and two static motion studies. Each panel has a comparison that removes a specific cue. Perceived effects vary with size, contrast and viewing conditions.

These are static drawings. The controls redraw them only when changed. Survey, references and integration plan · Leaf and coral structures · Hamonshū water catalogue

Geometric interference

Crossing currents

Two fine line families form broad bands. The shared bend carries their interference into an asymmetric water field.

Set bend to zero to examine straight gratings. For equal pitch 6 and angle δ, the ideal beat period is 6 / (2 sin(δ/2)); at δ = 0 there is no finite beat.

Organic interference

Veins inside a leaf

Two displaced fans of lines are clipped by a notched leaf. Broad structures emerge from their local intersections.

A ginkgo-inspired interference treatment. These straight fans are a decorative overlay; the botanical study shows forked veins. No single global beat period describes this field.

Perceived tilt

Straight lines, offset tiles

Offset light and dark tiles can make parallel mortar lines look tilted. This suggests an optical border around a regular hobnail field.

Gregory and Heard, 1979: the Café Wall effect depends on tile contrast, mortar width and mortar luminance.

Depth from shading

Faceted hobnail

Four face tones give a flat lattice a raised or recessed reading. Reversing the tones changes the lighting cue without moving any edges.

A pictorial depth cue, distinct from a measured geometric distortion. The control retains the same ridges and boundaries with uniform face tone.

Static motion study

Luminance around an eddy

Repeated dark-to-light ordering may create a peripheral drift impression around the rings. Nothing in the drawing moves.

Kitaoka and Ashida, 2003: luminance order and fragmented edges matter. This independently drawn study is not a reproduction of their artwork.

Relative motion study

A floating water medallion

The rectangular texture changes orientation inside the oval. Small viewing movements can produce an impression of relative motion.

Fermüller, Pless and Aloimonos, 2000: an account of the Ouchi effect. The comparison matches the inner and outer textures so the oval boundary disappears.
